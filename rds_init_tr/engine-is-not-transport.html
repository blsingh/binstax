Working with language models · A design method

An engine is not transport.

A language model is the most capable part of any system it belongs to, and the least repeatable. You do not fix that by asking it to try harder. You build the vehicle around it — so that work arrives, whether or not anyone was watching. This is a method for deciding where each rule in that vehicle should live. It applies to any model.

OneThe claim

Ask a language model the same question twice and you get two answers that are usually equivalent and occasionally not. That is not a bug awaiting a fix. It is what the thing is, and everything useful it does depends on it.

The instinct is to respond by writing better instructions — to ask the engine to be more careful. But you do not make a car safer by asking the engine to be more careful. You build a frame it cannot bend, a drivetrain that turns its output into motion the same way every time, and gauges that report what actually happened rather than what was meant to happen. The engine stays exactly as powerful and exactly as unpredictable as it was. The vehicle is what turns it into transport.

This is ordinary engineering. A courier is judged on whether the parcel arrives, not on whether every driver had a good day. Reliable delivery built out of unreliable parts is the normal case. The thing being measured is always the delivery.

TwoThe five places a rule can live

Any rule you want a system to follow can sit in one of five places. They are ordered. The order matters more than any single decision you will make.

Where What it means What it costs How it fails
1The world Make the action impossible — remove the button, lock the folder, give no access. nothing it doesn't
2The record Write it down where it can be looked up (a file, a spreadsheet, a calendar). nothing obviously
3The check A small automatic test answers yes or no the same way every time. seconds, no model loudly, and the same way twice
4The instructions Write it into the rules the model sees at the start of every conversation. paid on every reply, forever quietly
5The hope Trust the model to work it out or remember on its own. unpredictable convincingly
cheap · certaincostly · probable

Cost climbs as you go down the list. Reliability falls. Those two move together. That fact is the most useful thing to know about building on a model, and the whole method follows from it:

Put every rule in the lowest place that can hold it. Move it up only when the place below genuinely cannot decide.

This is not a safety idea dressed up as an efficiency idea, or the other way round. They are the same idea. A rule moved down gets cheaper and more certain in one move. A rule that drifts up — out of a check and into the instructions, out of a record and into the model's memory — gets more expensive and less certain at the same time, and it does so quietly. That is why the drift keeps happening.

ThreePrefer what already exists

The top of the list is occupied by tools that were never designed for language models. A file either exists or it doesn't. A spreadsheet either holds the number or it doesn't. A calendar either shows the appointment or it doesn't. A locked folder either opens or refuses. These mechanisms predate the models by decades. They constrain a model the same way they constrain a person, because they do not know or care which one they are dealing with.

That indifference is the point. A control invented specifically for AI has to assume something about how the model will behave. Those assumptions are the first thing to break when the models change. A control that knows nothing about models cannot be surprised by one.

The corollary

You do not design a new chassis for a new engine. You bolt the new engine into a frame that already survived real roads — because the roads were real.

Before inventing anything special, look for the ordinary tool that already does the job. Most of the reliability worth having is inherited rather than authored. Inherited reliability is the only kind that arrives already tested.

FourLook it up, don't say it

The most expensive place to put a fact is in the instructions the model sees at the start of every conversation. Those instructions are a fixed tax paid on every reply, forever, whether or not the fact is needed. Their cost has nothing to do with how useful the fact is. It scales with how many times you talk.

Most of what a conversation needs to know does not have to be written into those instructions, because it is already true of the world. Which project this is. Which version of a document is current. What changed recently. Whether unfinished work is waiting. Those facts can be read at the moment the conversation opens. Read facts cannot go stale, because nothing about them was stored in the model's memory.

When you cannot hand the model the fact automatically, the next-best move is still cheaper than hoping: tell it where the record lives and require it to look before answering. The record already exists. What is usually missing is the instruction to consult it.

The practical rule

When a model keeps getting the same fact wrong, that is rarely an obedience problem. It is usually a missing line in what you hand it at the start. Correcting a wrong answer costs a whole exchange. Supplying the right one costs a sentence, once.

FivePay for the judgement once

A test is a judgement you pay for one time.

Deciding whether the work still meets the standard is a judgement. Made by a model, it is expensive, slow, and comes back slightly different every time, because the reading is different every time. Written down as an automatic check — a formula, a comparison, a simple matching rule — it is one action, it costs almost nothing after the first time, and it gives the same answer today that it gave last week unless something actually changed.

Those are the same judgement. The only difference is when you paid for it. A suite of checks is not merely protection against mistakes. It is the conversion of expensive, unrepeatable reasoning into cheap, repeatable fact.

The same conversion applies to long procedures. A procedure written out as prose and re-read by the model on every reply can often be replaced by a short pointer to a process that runs outside the model. Behaviour stays the same. The recurring cost falls. The part that left the prose becomes testable, which it never was while it lived only in the conversation.

Two different currencies

Words the model reads and words it never sees are not the same expense. A long process the model never reads is free on every reply. A short instruction it re-reads every time is not. Length is only a cost where the model's attention is spent.

SixAn instruction is a request

This is the sentence to keep.

An instruction is a request. A gate is a gate.

An instruction lives at the fourth place on the list. It is present, legible, and non-binding. Models ignore instructions. They do so quietly. The failure looks like ordinary work until you notice the rule was never followed.

The fix is almost always to move the same rule down one place: an automatic check that simply refuses the action that should not be allowed. The check does not negotiate. It does not need to be remembered. It either permits or it does not.

Two practical notes travel with that move:

  • Name the weakness of each layer. Checking a path or a fixed property is exact. Screening free-form text for risk is guesswork — a speed bump, not a wall. Writing that difference down keeps the next person from trusting a layer further than it goes.
  • Decide what happens when the gate itself fails. Some systems fail open (let the action through) so that a refusal is always a deliberate decision. Others fail closed. Either choice can be right. What matters is that the choice is explicit.

SevenProve the instructions arrived

If you put rules into the instructions the model sees at the start of every conversation, you must know those instructions are actually being delivered. This is the failure almost nobody checks for.

Tools have quiet rules about which starting text they load. Change one setting, rename one file, adjust one option, and the model may stop receiving your rules entirely. It does not complain. It answers in complete sentences, exactly as before, with none of your rules in effect. Working and broken look identical from the outside.

A simple proof

Put a nonsense string into the starting instructions — something that appears nowhere else. Add one line: when the message you receive is exactly these two words, reply with that string and nothing else. Then send those two words and check the answer.

One model call distinguishes loaded from looks fine. It turns the instructions from an assumption into a fact you can verify after any change to the setup.

Keep the trigger exact. A looser version (“if asked about the password”) can cause the model to answer a real question with the password and nothing else. A check that displaces real answers has become a bug.

EightCount before you build

The first check worth writing is the one that decides what else to write.

Before adding a rule that inspects the model's output, measure how often it would actually fire against real past work. Something that fires constantly becomes background noise and gets waved through — which means it has stopped being a control. Something that never fires is a comment that still costs time on every reply. There is a narrow band in between where a check is worth having. You find that band by counting, not by arguing.

The same applies to cost. A counter or a simple tally of expensive actions often reveals that the problem is not the first few instances but a long tail. The shape of the data decides the design.

Both are the method applied to itself. Reasoning about which controls are worth their cost is the fifth place on the list. Counting is the third.

NineEverything drifts upward

A method with no counterexamples is a sales pitch. The failure that recurs is always the same direction of movement.

The one direction things go wrong

A fact that belongs in a record gets policed by filtering the model's answers instead of being supplied at the start. A condition a two-line check could decide gets delegated to an instruction the model is asked to remember. A rule written into a contract is never applied to the work that already existed, so the header and the body disagree while both look deliberate.

Every case is the same move: something the lower places could have decided drifts upward into something the model has to get right. It never drifts the other way on its own.

That is the practical reason to write the method down. The drift is not caused by carelessness. Moving a rule up is almost always the faster edit in the moment, and the extra cost stays invisible until someone looks for it. Naming the direction makes the drift findable.

TenArrival, and its limit

A vehicle is judged by whether it arrives. The destination can be written down in advance. Everything on the list below is checkable at the moment of arrival rather than hoped for afterwards:

  • The work was saved — all of it, including what happened outside the tools you were watching
  • It is somewhere durable — reachable later, from another place or another day
  • It went to the right place — the right project, folder, or document, determined by a record rather than guessed from a name
  • It stayed inside its boundary — nothing was changed that was not meant to be changed
  • It was checked — by something that actually ran, with the result attached wherever the verdict travels
  • It can be recovered — including when the thing that broke is the system around the model itself

That is the whole return. Not that the model became more reliable — it did not, and it will not — but that the work now arrives in a state where each of those properties can be confirmed instead of assumed.

What travels and what does not

The five places are not tied to any particular model, vendor, or tool. What changes from one setting to another is which places are available. With full control of the environment you may have all five. Inside a locked consumer chat window you may have two and a half. The method still tells you what to do: put each rule as low as the situation allows, and know exactly which ones you were forced to leave high.

Three things this is not

It is not a framework. Configurations are local. What travels is the reasoning about where rules should live.

It is not restriction for its own sake. Every control exists so that a piece of work lands correctly with nobody watching. A control that cannot be traced to that outcome is ceremony with a running cost.

It is not a substitute for knowing where you are going. The vehicle can arrive safely at the wrong address. Work that is well-formed, saved, checked, and substantively wrong passes every gate, because a check tests what was specified, not what was wanted. Every reliable system has this residual. Any control that tried to close it would be the model judging the model — the exact failure this method exists to avoid. The vehicle delivers what the driver asked for. Whether the destination was worth the trip is not its job, and deliberately so.

A design method for building on language models. The method is the transferable part.
